## Overview

**Speaker:** Dr. Tao Xu, Distinguished Professor, Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry, NIU  
**Date:** Friday, October 16, 2026, 3:30 PM  
**Room:** PM110

An electron carries mass, charge, and spin. Controlling charge gave us modern computing. Controlling spin may give us quantum information, smarter chemistry, and better ways to recover critical materials. Prof. Xu takes us across three frontiers:

- **Quantum information:** how a chemically tunable semiconductor enables spin–spin interactions, the basis of a newly funded DOE project on quantum coherence.
- **Chemical reactions:** how controlling spin can steer reactions, improving the conversion of CO₂ into useful chemicals using electricity.
- **An open challenge for AI:** rare-earth ions with nearly identical charge and size respond differently to magnetic environments. Could AI help design materials that separate elements conventional chemistry struggles to tell apart?

## Why This Talk

This isn't a talk about using an off-the-shelf model. It's a direct invitation from an experimental lab looking for AI collaborators. The questions on the table are ones ML researchers care about: Can algorithms uncover hidden interactions from noisy experimental signals? Can they guide which experiment to run next? Can they do inverse design, finding a material for a desired function? If you're looking for real scientific data, a new application area, or a thesis/project idea, this is a great chance to meet a potential collaborator. No chemistry or physics background is needed.

## About the Speaker

Dr. Tao Xu joined NIU in 2006 and is now a Distinguished Professor and Board of Trustees Professor. His group studies nanomaterials for energy conversion and storage, catalysis, quantum information science, and sensing. His honors include an NSF CAREER Award, a rare NSF Special Creativity Award, two R&D 100 Awards, and recognition as an Illinois "Researcher to Know" and "Innovator to Know." He received his Ph.D. from the University of Alabama and did postdoctoral work at Texas A&M University and Argonne National Laboratory.
